1    Introduction
The Berenstein conjecture belongs to the classical Pompeiu–Schiffer circle of rigidity problems,
which links rigid-motion integral transforms and Fourier zero sets to overdetermined Laplace
eigenvalue problems. Its integral-geometric origins lie in Pompeiu’s three papers of 1929 [29,
30, 31]. Brown, Schreiber, and Taylor placed the problem in a Fourier–spectral framework
in 1973, and Williams identified its equivalence, for domains homeomorphic to a ball, with
the overdetermined Neumann problem now associated with Schiffer [5, 39]. For the Pompeiu–
Schiffer problem, Garofalo and Segala used asymptotic methods for complexified Fourier
integrals and techniques from univalent-function theory to establish the Pompeiu property for
several classes of simply connected planar domains [16, 15, 17, 18]. Nigam, Siudeja, and Young
combined validated finite-element eigenvalue bounds with a nodal-line argument to prove that
the regular pentagon admits a nonconstant Neumann eigenfunction whose boundary trace is
strictly positive [28]. For further history and references, see [7]. These results concern the area
measure of the domain, rather than the boundary arclength measure arising in the Berenstein
problem.
     The Dirichlet problem has an old history. In his 1954 treatment of Rayleigh’s fixed-area
first Dirichlet eigenvalue problem, Schiffer derived constancy of the normal derivative as the
Euler–Lagrange condition at a stationary domain [34]. A 1957 lecture explicitly asked whether
this condition for the first eigenfunction characterises the disc [35, p. 132]. The unrestricted
rigidity question for an arbitrary, possibly sign-changing, Dirichlet eigenfunction is generally
attributed to Berenstein’s 1980 paper [2] and remained open for more than four decades.
     Let Ω ⊂ Rn be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. In its modern form, the
Berenstein conjecture asks whether the existence of k > 0 and a nonzero function u satisfying
              (∆ + k 2 )u = 0   in Ω,     u = 0,      ∂ν u = c   on ∂Ω,      c ̸= 0,           (1)
forces Ω to be a ball. Multiplying u by c−1 reduces to c = 1. Discs satisfy (1). If BR ⊂ R2
is the disc of radius R and j0,q is a positive zero of J0 , then u(r) = J0 (j0,q r/R) has zero
boundary trace and constant nonzero normal derivative. The conjecture asserts that there are
no other such domains. Serrin’s moving-plane theorem proves this when the eigenfunction
is strictly positive or strictly negative in the interior [36]; consequently any counterexample
must involve a higher, sign-changing Dirichlet eigenfunction.
    Beyond the sign-definite regime, the principal rigidity results either involve many spectral
parameters or require additional hypotheses. Berenstein proved that, in the Euclidean plane,
solvability for infinitely many spectral parameters forces the domain to be a disc; Berenstein
and Yang extended the corresponding rigidity to the Poincaré disc and then to arbitrary
Euclidean dimension [2, 3, 4]. Dalmasso later obtained finiteness and nonexistence results
for a broader problem with an affine source term, as well as low-eigenvalue rigidity for the
Berenstein problem under additional hypotheses [11, 12]. Liu obtained a criterion for the
Berenstein problem involving the second interior normal derivative, while Kawohl and Lucia
characterised related Schiffer-type problems in terms of the third, fourth, and fifth normal
derivatives on planar analytic boundaries [23, 22].
    For the linear eigenvalue problem, counterexamples were previously known on unbounded
periodic domains [10, 26]. For general semilinear equations, Ruiz showed that the positivity
hypothesis in Serrin’s theorem is essential by constructing a sign-changing solution on a
bounded domain other than a ball for a suitable nonlinearity [32]. For bounded convex centrally
symmetric planar domains with connected C 2,ε boundary, a recent preprint proves rigidity
whenever the corresponding eigenvalue exceeds an explicit domain-dependent threshold [9].
Our example is not centrally symmetric and therefore lies outside that class.
    Colbrook and Stepaniants recently gave the first counterexamples to the planar Pompeiu
and Schiffer conjectures [7]. The conformal fixed-disc, disk-polynomial, and finite-block/infinite-

1.1   Boundary arclength and the Fourier circle
The boundary condition in (3) has a direct harmonic-analytic reformulation. Let σΓ be
arclength measure on a Jordan curve Γ. We prove in Proposition 2.1 that, for a C 2 bounded
simply connected domain,

               (1) holds for the frequency k     ⇐⇒       σd

The forward implication is Green’s identity. For the converse, the outgoing single-layer
potential with constant density has zero far field, hence vanishes in the exterior by Rellich
uniqueness; the normal-derivative jump then produces the required interior eigenfunction.
Thus Theorem 1.1 also gives a noncircular analytic Jordan curve whose arclength Fourier
transform vanishes on the circle {ξ ∈ R2 : |ξ| = k}.
    The Fourier equations provide an effective search mechanism, but they are not well suited
to uniform tail estimates. The proof therefore proceeds through the fixed-disc system, whose
high-mode linearisation has a diagonal, isometric principal part and admits explicit monotone
bounds.

1.2   The Pompeiu–Schiffer framework and the new Dirichlet endpoint
The two constructions arise at complementary endpoints of mixed overdetermined Helmholtz
problems. Wheeler constructed local noncircular branches with both boundary constants
nonzero [38]. Colbrook and Stepaniants worked in the 10-fold symmetry class and continued
to the endpoint at which the Neumann datum vanishes, yielding the Pompeiu–Schiffer
counterexample [7]. The present D13 construction instead reaches the endpoint at which the
Dirichlet datum vanishes. At the Fourier level, [7] concerns cancellation of the area measure
1Ω , whereas the present problem concerns cancellation of the boundary arclength measure
σ∂Ω .
    The distinction becomes more pronounced after conformal transfer to the disc. In the
Schiffer problem the decomposition U = 1 + v gives both v|T = 0 and ∂r v|T = 0. Consequently
g = ∆v lies in the range compatible with both vanishing traces, its harmonic coordinates
disappear, and an inverse K of the Laplacian on this range absorbs the boundary conditions.
With pS := kϕ′ as in [7], the problem reduces to the single cubic equation

5.2   Recovery of the boundary condition
The geometric argument leaves only the sign lost on squaring the boundary equation. Let
U∗ = KD g∗ . Proposition 3.4 gives
                                         ∆U∗ = g∗ ,        U∗ |T = 0.
The first fixed-disc equation gives
                                          ∆U∗ + k∗2 |a∗ |2 U∗ = 0.
The second equation gives (N g∗ )2 = |a∗ |2 . By (59),
                              inf N g∗ (eiθ ) ≥ 0.6058177124928927 > 0,
                             θ∈R

while (58) implies that a∗ has no zeros. Hence
                                           N g∗ = |a∗ |     on T.
Proposition 3.5 therefore applies with ε = 1, yielding u∗ = U∗ ◦ ϕ−1∗ as a solution of (3). The
coefficient ball also gives |k∗ − k ◦ | ≤ 10−6 . which together with (35), implies the outward-
rounded interval (2).

5.3   Analytic regularity and sign change
The analytic-continuation argument of [7, Sec. 4.3] applies here with
                                   c∗ (x, y) := k∗2 |a∗ (x + iy)|2 .
Fix q > 2. By Proposition 3.4, U∗ ∈ W 2,q (D) ∩ W01,q (D). The coefficient c∗ is real analytic
on a neighbourhood of D, and interior analytic elliptic regularity gives U∗ ∈ C ω (D) [27].
Since a∗ has no zeros, |a∗ | is positive and real analytic on a collar of T. Smooth boundary
regularity gives U∗ ∈ C ∞ (D) [1]. The Cauchy–Kowalevski theorem, applied in analytic
boundary coordinates to
                       e |T = 0,
                       U               ∂r U
                                          e       = |a∗ |,   (∆ + c∗ )U
                                                                      e = 0,
                                              T
produces a real-analytic solution on a two-sided collar of T; see also [25]. The zero-extension
and analytic-uniqueness step in [7, Sec. 4.3], now with these Cauchy data, identifies U e with
U∗ on the inner half-collar. Thus U∗ extends real analytically across T.
    Since ϕ∗ is univalent on a complex neighbourhood of D, its inverse is holomorphic on a
neighbourhood of Ω, after shrinking the neighbourhoods if necessary. Consequently
                                    u∗ = U∗ ◦ ϕ−1   ω
                                               ∗ ∈ C (Ω).

   The eigenfunction u∗ must change sign. Otherwise it would be a first Dirichlet eigenfunction
and would have a strict sign in the interior. After changing its sign if necessary, Serrin’s
theorem would force Ω to be a disc [36], contradicting the preceding noncircularity argument.
Proof of Theorem 1.1. Theorem 4.6 and the reconstruction above give a bounded simply
connected D13 -invariant domain with real-analytic Jordan boundary, the frequency interval
(2), and a real-analytic solution of (3). The nonzero coefficient (a∗ )1 shows that the do-
main is neither a disc nor centrally symmetric, and Serrin’s theorem gives the sign change.
Proposition 2.1 gives (4). This is a counterexample to the planar Berenstein conjecture.
