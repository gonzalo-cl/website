Schiffer's conjecture · Pompeiu's conjecture

The planar Schiffer and Pompeiu counterexamples

The planar Schiffer conjecture asserted that a bounded simply connected domain admitting an overdetermined eigenfunction must be a disc. The two Schiffer papers presented here give counterexamples by different rigorous arguments. The computer-assisted route has also been used to disprove the Berenstein conjecture.

The boundary-value problem

The disc is not the only Schiffer domain.

Both Schiffer papers construct a bounded, simply connected, noncircular domain \(\Omega\subset\mathbb R^2\) and a nonconstant function \(u\) satisfying the same Cauchy data on the whole boundary.

\[ \begin{aligned} (\Delta+k^2)u&=0 &&\text{in }\Omega,\\ u&=1,\qquad \partial_\nu u=0 &&\text{on }\partial\Omega. \end{aligned} \]

01

History and formulations

One problem in three forms

Pompeiu's integral question, a Fourier zero condition, and Schiffer's boundary-value problem are linked formulations. For the domains considered here, a Schiffer solution gives the Fourier zero circle and hence failure of the Pompeiu property.

  1. 1929

    Pompeiu's integral problem

    Pompeiu asked whether a planar set can be recognised from the integrals of a function over all translated and rotated copies of that set.

  2. 1944

    The disc exception

    Chakalov observed that a disc fails the property. Its Fourier transform is radial, and a positive zero of \(J_1\) gives an entire circle of Fourier zeros.

  3. 1973–76

    Fourier and spectral reformulations

    Brown, Schreiber and Taylor gave the Fourier–Laplace characterisation; Williams related it to the overdetermined eigenvalue problem now associated with Schiffer.

  4. 2026

    Planar counterexamples

    The two Schiffer papers above construct bounded simply connected noncircular domains, one by a computer-assisted proof and the other by bifurcation theory. A companion computer-assisted paper treats the complementary Berenstein problem.

Integral formulation

Failure of the Pompeiu property

\[\int_{\sigma(\Omega)} f(x)\,\mathrm dx=0.\]

Here \(f\not\equiv0\), and the equality holds for every rigid motion \(\sigma\).

Fourier formulation

A zero circle

\[\widehat{\mathbf 1_\Omega}(k\omega)=0\qquad(\omega\in S^1).\]

The Fourier transform of the indicator of \(\Omega\) vanishes on a complete circle of radius \(k\).

Spectral formulation

The Schiffer problem

\[ \begin{aligned} -\Delta u&=\lambda u &&\text{in }\Omega,\\ u&=1,\quad \partial_\nu u=0 &&\text{on }\partial\Omega. \end{aligned} \]

The boundary value and normal derivative are prescribed simultaneously, so solutions occur only at special domains and frequencies.

The obstruction at the disc

Direct nonradial bifurcation is spectrally blocked.

Linearising at a disc shows that a genuine nonradial deformation would require two distinct integer-order Bessel functions to share a positive zero. They do not. The remaining kernel directions are the geometric changes coming from translation and dilation.

\[J_1(\rho)=0,\qquad J_n(\rho)=0,\qquad n\ne1.\]

The first paper solves the full nonlinear equation near a computed domain. The second temporarily allows the Bessel order to be real.

02

Colbrook · Stepaniants

The computer-assisted proof

The moving boundary is encoded by a conformal map of the unit disc. The PDE and both boundary conditions then become one equation in a weighted coefficient space, where an a posteriori contraction argument proves the existence of an exact zero.

Main theorem

There is a bounded, simply connected, noncircular \(D_{10}\)-symmetric Schiffer domain with real-analytic boundary.

The numerical approximation locates the construction. The theorem follows from exact identities, interval enclosures, exhaustive finite enumeration, analytic tail bounds, and a final exact-rational check.

How the centre is found. Before validation, numerical continuation solves the related constant-flux problem \(\partial_\nu u=c\), starting from a known solution with \(c\ne0\) and following the branch towards \(c=0\). This produces the approximate conformal coefficients. It does not establish the theorem: the proof begins by bounding the residual of the fixed-disc equation and then closes the full-space certificate described below.

1

Put the unknown domain on the unit disc.

A ten-fold symmetric conformal map is written as

\[\phi_p(z)=p_0z+\sum_{j\ge1}p_jz^{10j+1}.\]

The coefficients \(p_j\) describe the boundary. Pulling the PDE back by \(\phi_p\) replaces the moving domain by a fixed disc and introduces the coefficient \(|\phi_p'|^2\).

2

Build both boundary conditions into the inverse.

Write \(g=\Delta(U-1)\). An explicit compatible inverse \(K\) is chosen so that \(Kg\) and its radial derivative vanish on the unit circle. Thus

\[U=1+Kg\]

already has the required Dirichlet and Neumann traces. This is the reason for the disk-polynomial basis used in the paper.

3

Solve one coefficient equation.

Substitution into the pulled-back Helmholtz equation gives a cubic equation

\[F(g,p)=0.\]

The weighted coefficient norm is a Banach algebra. It controls multiplication, uniform convergence on the closed disc, and the analytic tails omitted from the finite computation.

4

Validate a contraction on the full space.

A numerical approximation supplies the centre and a finite approximate inverse. The proof joins this finite block to analytic tail estimates and bounds the Newton-like map \(T(x)=x-\mathcal A F(x)\). The released certificate produces

\[\mathcal R(t)=Y+(Z-1)t+C_2t^2+C_3t^3.\]

At \(r=10^{-6}\), the strict inequalities \(\mathcal R(r)<0\) and \(\mathcal R'(r)<0\) make the coefficient ball invariant and contractive.

5

Return to a physical domain.

The certified coefficient bounds prove that the conformal map is injective, extends analytically across the unit circle, and is not a linear map. The exact coefficient zero therefore reconstructs a noncircular real-analytic domain carrying the required Schiffer solution.

What is checked beyond the plotted polynomial?

The finite derivative block has 2,471 variables. The certificate also reconstructs every omitted residual row, enumerates the adjacent field-source and shape tails, and bounds the remote tails by monotone analytic estimates. Directed MPFR interval computations produce the leaves; a separate exact-rational checker derives the final inequalities and the geometric reconstruction bounds.

Companion computer-assisted result · Colbrook · Sadeghi · Stepaniants

The complementary Dirichlet endpoint

The conformal fixed-disc and validated-tail framework was subsequently adapted to the planar Berenstein conjecture. Here the boundary datum that vanishes is exchanged:

\[ \begin{aligned} (\Delta+k^2)u&=0 &&\text{in }\Omega,\\ u&=0,\qquad \partial_\nu u=1 &&\text{on }\partial\Omega. \end{aligned} \]

The planar Berenstein conjecture asserted that these zero Dirichlet and constant nonzero Neumann data force \(\Omega\) to be a disc. In Fourier language, the cancellation now concerns boundary arclength measure \(\sigma_{\partial\Omega}\), rather than the area measure \(\mathbf 1_\Omega\,dx\) in the Pompeiu formulation.

The paper constructs a bounded simply connected \(D_{13}\)-symmetric domain with real-analytic Jordan boundary and a solution \(u\in C^\omega(\overline\Omega)\), at a frequency satisfying \(27.4381178838<k<27.4381198839\). The domain is neither a disc nor centrally symmetric, and \(u\) changes sign. The sign change is essential: a solution of one sign would fall under Serrin's symmetry theorem. Equivalently, \(\widehat{\sigma_{\partial\Omega}}(k\omega)=0\) for every \(\omega\in\mathbb S^1\).

What changes, and how it is handled

Set \(a=\phi'\), \(U=u\circ\phi\), and \(g=\Delta U\). The full zero-Dirichlet inverse \(K_D\) and its Neumann trace \(N\) give the coupled fixed-disc system

\[ \begin{aligned} g+k^2|a|^2K_Dg&=0,\\ (Ng)^2-|a|^2&=0. \end{aligned} \]
The source space is larger.
For Schiffer, both traces of \(U-1\) vanish and the harmonic source modes disappear, leaving the single equation \(g+|p_{\mathrm S}|^2(1+Kg)=0\). The nonzero Berenstein flux keeps those modes, so the proof constructs and bounds both \(K_D\) and \(N\).
The boundary condition is a second equation.
Squaring \(Ng=|a|\) makes the system polynomial but also admits the wrong sign. A certified positive lower bound for \(Ng\) recovers the unsquared condition.
Global extension must be retained.
Analytic Cauchy data determine a solution near the boundary, but not necessarily throughout the enclosed region. The \(K_D\)-system constructs the solution on the whole disc; a separate continuation argument extends it analytically across the unit circle.
The finite-tail split is rebuilt.
The trace \(N\) annihilates every disk-polynomial mode with radial index \(s\ge1\); only the harmonic \(s=0\) modes contribute. Exact support separation then joins an 882-variable finite block to analytic tail bounds and a full-space contraction certificate.

Cao-Labora · de Dios Pont

The bifurcation proof

The proof changes the parameter in which bifurcation is performed. Rotational order is first allowed to be real, giving access to Bessel crossings that are unavailable at integer order. Uniform estimates then show that selected branches return to an integer.

Main theorem

There are infinitely many bounded, simply connected, noncircular star-shaped Schiffer domains with real-analytic boundaries.

Their rotational orders are unbounded. Each planar domain is obtained only after the auxiliary real-order branch reaches an integer value.

1

Quotient by rotational symmetry.

An \(N\)-fold planar domain is reduced to one fundamental sector. After rescaling its angular coordinate to period \(2\pi\), the quotient carries the cone metric

\[g_R=dr^2+\frac{r^2}{R^2}\,d\psi^2.\]

For a planar quotient \(R=N\) is an integer, but the equation and metric make sense for real \(R\).

2

Work on a fixed collar without discarding the core.

A neighbourhood of the boundary is straightened to \([0,L]\times\mathbb T\). The omitted interior is represented exactly by its Dirichlet-to-Neumann map. On the cosine mode \(k\),

\[\begin{aligned}\mathcal D_R[\cos(k\psi)]&=\tau_k(R)\cos(k\psi),\\z_R&=\sqrt\lambda(R-L),\\\tau_k(R)&=\sqrt\lambda\,\frac{J'_{kR}(z_R)}{J_{kR}(z_R)}.\end{aligned}\]

This interface condition matches both the value and normal derivative, so a collar solution extends regularly through the cone point.

3

Bifurcate at a real Bessel crossing.

Fix a zero \(\rho\) of \(J_1\). A real order \(R_*\) is chosen so that

\[J_1(\rho)=J_{R_*}(\rho)=0.\]

The linearised collar equation then has a one-dimensional even kernel. Transversality gives a local branch \((R(s),v(s))\) with first boundary wave \(v'(0)=\Phi_*(y)\cos\psi\).

4

Prove that every selected branch moves down in order.

Symmetry gives \(R'(0)=0\). The cubic solvability calculation and uniform Bessel estimates give a negative second derivative, while uniform \(C^3\) control bounds the remainder:

\[\begin{aligned}R(s)&=R_*+\frac12R''(0)s^2+O(|s|^3),\\R''(0)&\le -c<0.\end{aligned}\]

The constants and the size of the branch are uniform along the required sequence of large crossings.

5

Choose a crossing just above an integer and lift it.

Uniform Debye asymptotics reduce the location of large real-order zeros to a phase condition. An irrational rotation argument supplies crossings \(R_*=N+\delta\) with arbitrarily small \(\delta>0\). The uniform decrease along the branch exceeds this gap, so continuity gives a point with \(R(s_N)=N\).

At integer order, exactly \(N\) copies of the quotient close in the plane. The nonzero first boundary harmonic makes the reconstructed domain noncircular.

What turns the quadratic picture into a proof?

The paper proves one branch size and one definite decrease that work uniformly for all sufficiently large crossings in a fixed spectral window. Separately, its near-integer proposition uses uniform Debye asymptotics and density modulo one to find crossings closer to an integer than that decrease. The intermediate-value theorem then produces the required integer point on each selected branch.

The same boundary-value problem, closed by different arguments

Both Schiffer papers begin by reformulating the moving-boundary problem and end by reconstructing a planar domain. The middle of each proof is different.
